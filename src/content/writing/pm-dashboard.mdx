In special education, data collection often happens long after a lesson ends. I might try to recall who met a learning outcome, who needed a prompt, and who was sitting quietly in the back. But relying on memory at the end of the day usually leads to vague records — and vague data makes writing meaningful IEP progress updates an uphill battle.

I wanted a system that would let me record direct observations *in the moment*, without pulling me away from teaching. So I decided to *try* something new, and I built a custom Progress Monitoring Dashboard.

## The design: data entry at the speed of teaching

The goal was simple: design an interface that lets me track three core metrics per student during small- or whole-group instruction.

<ul class="metric-list">
  <li>
    <span class="tag">GOAL</span>
    <span><strong>Goal Progress.</strong> Whether a student met their target learning objective for the day.</span>
  </li>
  <li>
    <span class="tag">INDEP</span>
    <span><strong>Independence Level.</strong> How independently the student completed the task versus requiring heavy prompting.</span>
  </li>
  <li>
    <span class="tag">INTERV</span>
    <span><strong>Intervention Level.</strong> The frequency or tier of supports provided (scale 0–5).</span>
  </li>
</ul>

Alongside those quick dropdowns, each student card includes a text area for rapid, real-time observational notes — like capturing behavioral triggers or physical complaints right as they happen.

## How it changes IEP writing

Having clean, time-stamped data logged *during* class changes the entire reporting process.

### 1. No more guesswork

Instead of guessing how often a student needed support over a nine-week period, I have structured records. I can see exact trends in goal mastery alongside independence metrics.

### 2. Concrete evidence for meetings

When discussing progress with parents, administrators, or IEP teams, I don't just say, "They're doing better." I can export a CSV report or pull up the dashboard to show specific dates, direct observations, and intervention counts.

### 3. Clearer next steps

By separating target goal performance from independence levels, I can quickly tell whether a student understands the material but still needs prompting, or whether they need an adjusted instructional entry point.

---

Nothing about this replaces judgment, or the annual paperwork, or the quarterly benchmarks. It isn't trying to. It's a teacher tool — the point is to make Sunday-night planning faster and Monday-morning teaching sharper.
